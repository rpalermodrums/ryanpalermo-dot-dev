Your alarm wakes you with a pleasant new song you've never heard before, just like it does every morning. Your "Discover Weekly" playlist—twenty-five tracks, all perfectly competent—guides you through your routine. The production is clean. The chord progressions resolve exactly as expected. The vocals sit perfectly in the mix.

You scroll through Instagram during breakfast: fantasy landscapes, portraits of beautiful people lit with unimaginable perfection, surreal compositions that would once have taken weeks to produce. Conjured effortlessly by someone who has never held a camera.

It's a beautiful world. A world of unlimited content (a word now interchangeable with *art*). A world so streamlined that it's unimaginable how people "used to live that way."

Welcome to the slop future.

---

Nobody asked for this. Not the artists, obviously. Not the audience either. Not one person woke up today wishing for music generated by components in a data center, wishing to see a painting that felt just a little less human, wishing for a book with less of the author's voice in it.

So why are we building it?

To get to the root, we have to go back to 2022–2023, when generative AI entered public consciousness with ChatGPT. I felt the disruption from both sides: as someone who loves building software, and as someone for whom music has defined my life.

From the tech side, what I saw wasn't simply market disruption. I watched deliberate choices made by companies, communities, and individuals—choices that systematically alienated the people who had dedicated their lives to creative work.

The worlds of generative AI and art grew adversarial almost immediately.

---

### Copyright

Let's start with the obvious one.

The first models powering AI image generation—Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, DALL-E—were trained on billions of images scraped from the internet. Many were copyrighted works by living artists. As someone who grew up in the Napster/LimeWire age (sorry to date myself), it feels hypocritical to say this, but it was a fair grievance. None of the artists consented to their work being used. None were paid or notified.

Meanwhile, in machine learning, breakthroughs were happening around the "transformers" architecture. A few research papers from Google DeepMind premiered an elegantly simple technical solution that would lead to "AI" as most people know it today. The researchers weren't purporting to be artists; they were engineers. Great ones. But building a capable model was untenable without massive training data, and the internet's vast, largely unauthorized trove of text, images, video, and metadata made incubation possible.

Whether those engineers acted ethically or not, artists felt—justifiably—violated. Early on, much of the value proposition depended on ingesting the sum total of human online content.

When artists objected, they were met with a familiar defense: "It's just like how humans learn."

I tried that argument once with my mother, an author. I remember the look she gave me. It was an "are you fucking stupid?" kind of look, but she was nice enough not to say that.

Anyway, I've come around to believing it's bullshit.

When a human studies another artist's work, they're building neural pathways through practice and observation. Influence gets filtered through lived experience, personal taste, and conscious choice. And we can only produce work at human speed. The meaning formed through lived experience outside the "training data" is what creates great art in the human sense.

When a model is trained, it encodes statistical patterns that can be reproduced at infinite scale. The output is derived mathematically, with the artist's contribution laundered through enough linear algebra to provide plausible deniability.

Artists figured this out quickly through experimentation. Writers asked ChatGPT to write like them and found uncanny resemblance, even in the GPT-3.5 era (two years ago, which already feels like ancient history). Years spent developing a voice felt reduced to a prompt modifier: "in the style of [your name here]." No attribution. No compensation. No recourse.

The message was clear: *Your work is our training data. Your style is a feature of our product. We're entitled to it, and you can't do anything about it.*

This dynamic hasn't changed much since.

---

### Framing

The copyright issue was bad enough. Then came the marketing.

The phrase "AI Artist" appeared almost immediately. *AI artist™*, as if typing the correct sequence of words at a model were a meaningful creative skill.

This wasn't an accident. The framing worked: it elevated the user while erasing the source of their production. The artists whose work filled the training data? Lame. Boomers. Replaced by a new kind of *artist™*—one without the labor, discipline, or skill.

When artists pushed back, they were labeled luddites, gatekeepers, and elitists.

The generative models themselves are brilliant, likely world-changing technology. The so-called "AI artists™" are not relevant to that achievement. (Aside from serving as free testers and telemetry sources.)

---

### Antagonism

If the copyright betrayal was the original sin and the framing war was the cover-up, what followed was uglier: a community that seemed to enjoy the conflict.

I watched it happen in real time. "AI art enthusiasts" would post generated images in art communities specifically to provoke. They'd seek out working artists and tell them their careers were over. When artists expressed grief, anger, or fear about their livelihoods, the response was "cope and seethe"—a phrase that became a rallying cry.

I couldn't say this more clearly: fuck all the way off.

The original "AI artists™" were not people with technical or artistic skill. They didn't build these models. They couldn't create a single piece of art in the training set.

But they were a viable user base.

AI art became a tribal identity, indistinguishable from memecoin culture. Not a tool someone used, but a flag they flew. How miserable.

This matters because culture shapes what gets built. When a community celebrates the harm a technology causes, that harm gets baked into the product. When "artists are mad" becomes a selling point, you get tools optimized to make artists mad.

We did.

---

That's where things stood by mid-2023: artists alienated, communities poisoned, battle lines drawn. The tech kept improving. The hostility kept growing. And both sides settled into positions that made the other side's worst assumptions true.

But here's the thing I keep coming back to: none of this was inevitable. The technology didn't require this outcome. People made choices, and those choices created the mess we're in.

Which raises a question I'll dig into in Part II: **What would it have looked like if we'd built this differently?**
