I played music professionally for about a decade before I wrote a single line of code. Drums, plus years of composing and arranging. Gigs, sessions, practice rooms, teaching students, late nights with notation software.

People ask what transferred to engineering and why musicians often make great devs. They expect me to say something about creativity, or pattern recognition, or collaboration. And sure, those things are true. But they're not really the point.

What actually carried over is simpler and impossible to teach: the disposition to sit alone with something difficult and build your own systems to get through it.

That, and taste.

## The Practice Room

Jazz, and music more broadly, is not learned in a classroom. It's learned in a practice room, alone, for thousands of hours.

No one is checking your work, no one gives you a curriculum. You listen to records. You transcribe solos. You play patterns at varying tempos until they're unconscious. You hit plateaus that feel like dead ends, sometimes for weeks or months, where nothing improves and you can't figure out why.

And then you figure it out. Or you push through anyway. Or you build a new system for yourself, a different way of practicing or hearing or approaching the problem, and suddenly you're moving again (eventually).

Most people don't have this. They learn what they're taught, plateau at "good enough," and stop. They need external structure, external feedback, external motivation to keep going.

Musicians learn to generate all of that internally. They have to. No one's coming to save you in the practice room. You either figure it out or you quit.

Engineering is the same, at least when you're working on problems worth solving. There's no tutorial. The plateaus are real. The path forward is rarely obvious. What carries over from music isn't any specific skill. It's the tolerance for being stuck and the stubborn belief that persistence will eventually pay off.

It does. It just takes longer than anyone wants.

## Taste

Here's what I think separates product engineers from software engineers: taste.

Software engineering is about making things work. Product engineering is about making things right. And "right" is not a technical specification. It's a judgment call. It's aesthetic.

The path a jazz musician follows teaches this directly. You can play a solo that's technically perfect. Correct changes, clean execution, sophisticated note choices, rhythm that frames the form. And the room stays dead. Something's missing. The feel is off.

Then someone plays three notes with conviction and people lean in.

What changed? Not the musical content. The experience of receiving it. The timing. The confidence. The way those three notes made people feel something, even if they couldn't explain what.

This is the gap that matters in product work too. Users don't experience your feature as a collection of technical decisions. They experience it as a feeling. Does this feel fast? Does this feel trustworthy? Does this feel like it's for someone like me? These aren't rational questions. The answers live somewhere beneath conscious evaluation.

You can build something that's correct by every measurable standard and still have it feel "wrong". And you can break half the "rules" and ship something that just works, that people immediately understand, that feels right in a way nobody can quite articulate.

Taste is the ability to tell the difference. It's not teachable in the traditional sense. You develop it by exposure. Thousands of hours of listening, playing, writing, and internalizing what lands until the judgment becomes automatic.

## Art, Not Science

There's a persistent fantasy in tech that building products is a science. That if you follow the right process, run the right experiments, collect the right data, the correct answer will emerge.

I don't think that's true. And I think the belief causes a lot of damage.

Data tells you what happened. It doesn't tell you what to do next. A/B tests tell you which of two options performed better. They don't tell you if both options were wrong, or if there's a third option you never considered. They definitely don't tell you why one option won, which means they can't tell you whether that reason will hold up in a different context.

We tend to measure what's easy to measure and then pretend that's what matters. Click rates. Conversion percentages. Time on page. These capture something, but they miss the texture of how people actually experience a product. They miss meaning.

Sometimes the "irrational" choice is the right one. Sometimes making something slightly harder to use makes it feel more valuable. Sometimes removing features adds clarity. Sometimes the thing that tests worse in isolation works better in context because of what it signals, how it frames everything around it.

Jazz musicians understand this intuitively. You can't A/B test a solo in real-time. You can't run experiments to determine the optimal voicing for a chord progression outside of the practice room. You don't get another shot at meeting the moment as you wish you had. Music optimized for any single measurable dimension would be soulless. The best performances work because of how all the elements feel together, not because each element was independently optimized.

Product engineering is the same kind of art. It requires vision, judgment, and the willingness to make calls that can't be fully justified by data. It requires the confidence to trust perception over measurement when they conflict.

The practice room teaches you to be comfortable with that uncertainty. You develop your ear, you make the call, you see what happens. Over and over, for years, until you trust yourself.

## Obsession and Standards

Let's be honest about what it takes to be a musician.

It's not talent alone. Talent gets you in the door. After that, it's hours, practice. Thousands upon thousands of hours of focused, deliberate practice. Listening to the same solo hundreds of times to catch a detail you missed, then slowing it down and learning that you misheard it all. Rewriting the same eight bars of an arrangement until they finally breathe. Playing a rudiment until your hands hurt as much as your brain, giving up, then doing the same thing the next day.

This is obsession. It's not healthy in the conventional sense. It's not balanced. It's what the work requires.

And alongside obsession lives standards. High standards. The refusal to accept "good enough" when you know it could be better. The willingness to throw away work that doesn't meet the bar, even when you've already spent hours, days, months on it. The uncomfortable honesty required to hear your own playing clearly and admit when it's not there yet.

I see this in the best engineers I've worked with. They're obsessed. They care about details no one asked them to care about. They refactor code that already works because it bothers them. They stay up late because something feels off and they can't let it go.

And they have standards. They know what good looks like. They won't ship work they're not proud of. They push back when the easy path means cutting corners.

This combination is rare. It's uncomfortable to be around sometimes. It produces great work.

Music selects for it. If you don't have it, you won't survive the practice room. If you do, you've already proven you can apply it to something difficult for years on end.

## The Plateau

Every musician hits plateaus. Long stretches where nothing improves, where the work feels pointless, where quitting seems like the rational move.

The plateau is the test. Not of talent. Of character.

Can you keep showing up when there's no visible progress? Can you build new systems for yourself when the old ones stop working? Can you trust that the work is accumulating even when you can't feel it?

Most people can't. They need constant feedback, constant progress, constant proof that the effort is worth it. When the plateau hits, they bail.

Musicians who make it through learn something important: persistence is not about willpower. It's about building systems that sustain you when willpower runs out.

You find new ways to practice. You set smaller goals. You change your relationship to the work so the process itself becomes satisfying, not just the results. You learn to trust the accumulation even when it's invisible.

This is exactly what long-term product work requires. Shipping takes forever. Feedback is delayed and noisy. The plateau is constant. If you need immediate gratification, you won't last.

The practice room is years of training for exactly this kind of patience. By the time you switch to engineering, the plateau is familiar. You know it doesn't mean you're failing. You know how to get through it. You've done it before. You learn to love that specific and familiar pain which is fully unfamiliar to those who had a clear path with external validation.

## What Survives

The skill that transfers from music to product engineering isn't music theory or rhythm or any specific technique.

It's the willingness to sit alone with something difficult and figure it out. It's the taste developed through obsessive exposure. It's the understanding that how something feels matters more than how it measures. It's the high standards and the refusal to ship something you're not proud of.

These aren't skills you learn from a book, but rather dispositions you develop through years of a particular kind of creative work.

The time spent in the practice room was that work. If you've already survived it, you're more prepared than you realize.
